## 1. The Relatable Opening Persona Story: The Busy Wholesale Depot That Was Losing Bulk Contracts

At 7:15 AM on a humid Tuesday morning in Ikeja Industrial Estate, the main distribution yard of *KoraTech Industrial & Electrical Supplies Ltd.* is already roaring with activity. Forklifts weave between towering steel racks stacked with armoured cables, industrial switchgear, inverter batteries, and boxed lighting fixtures. Outside the concrete loading bay, three 30-ton trucks bound for Ibadan, Onitsha, and Abuja idle while loaders haul sealed cartons onto flatbed trailers.

Overseeing this operation—alongside a high-velocity wholesale trade branch inside Alaba International Market—is Bayo, a seasoned warehouse and logistics director with fifteen years in Nigerian heavy distribution. Bayo knows every major electrical contractor, sub-distributor, and regional stockist across Southern Nigeria by first name. As reflected in Punch Nigeria's reporting on rigorous cargo inspections at Lagos ports (featured photo above), moving bulk commercial merchandise through Lagos corridors requires precision, regulatory clarity, and airtight chain-of-custody records from port clearance to final depot dispatch.

*But Bayo has a problem.*

Despite strong market demand and decades of supplier goodwill, *KoraTech* has hit an infuriating operational ceiling. Every time monthly volume climbs past fifty heavy dispatches a week, the entire depot grinds into administrative gridlock. The problem is not inventory financing or customer demand. The problem is that Bayo's multi-million-Naira wholesale enterprise still runs on carbon-copy waybill booklets, laminated price sheets, handwritten pallet bin-cards, and chaotic WhatsApp voice notes.

Every single week, Bayo's distribution operation suffers three expensive operational micro-failures that bleed margin and hand loyal sub-distributors to faster rivals:

### 1. The 48-Hour Pro-Forma Quotation Delay
At 9:10 AM on Tuesday, a major Abuja construction procurement officer calls Bayo's sales desk requesting an urgent pro-forma invoice for forty-five industrial circuit breakers, two hundred rolls of armoured cable, and eighty distribution boards, delivered to Gwagwalada by Friday.

Because volume discount tiers live inside Bayo's head and actual pallet counts sit on physical bin-cards spread across the Ikeja depot and Alaba annex, the sales clerk cannot quote immediately. She calls the Ikeja floor supervisor to count pallets by hand, waits three hours for Alaba to check their ledger book, and chases a haulage broker by phone for current diesel-adjusted interstate freight rates. By the time Bayo finally signs the PDF quotation on Wednesday afternoon, the procurement officer has already issued the ₦28,000,000 purchase order to a digitized rival who delivered a verified quote in twenty minutes.

### 2. The Phantom Pallet and Bin-Card Mismatch
At 1:40 PM, a regular sub-distributor from Benin City arrives with a hired van after paying a deposit based on Bayo's paper ledger showing 120 cartons of solar inverters in stock. When the forklift operator drives down Aisle 4, only 74 cartons remain on the rack.

Forty-six cartons had been dispatched to a walk-in Alaba trader the previous afternoon, but the yellow carbon-copy tally sheet had not yet been carried upstairs to the accounting desk. The sub-distributor's truck sits idle for five hours accruing standby waiting charges while Bayo's team scrambles to source replacement units from neighboring wholesalers at razor-thin margins.

### 3. The Missing Waybill and Disputed Freight Surcharge
On Friday evening, Bayo sits in his glass-walled warehouse office surrounded by a knee-high stack of dog-eared triplicate waybills, smudged driver gate passes, and handwritten weighbridge slips. A regional buyer in Onitsha refuses to clear his 30-day credit balance of ₦6,400,000, insisting that twelve cartons arrived damaged and that an extra interstate transit surcharge was never approved.

Because the signed paper proof-of-delivery sheet was rain-soaked inside the truck cabin and the driver's phone photos were deleted, Bayo has zero verifiable chain-of-custody evidence. He spends four exhausting hours arguing over the phone and eventually writes off ₦850,000 just to keep the account alive.

His business has deep inventory, prime Ikeja and Alaba depots, and loyal B2B buyers. What it lacks is a connected system.
